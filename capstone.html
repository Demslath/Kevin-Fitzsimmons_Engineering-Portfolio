Capstone design, McGill University, 2025

Digestate to Clean Water

An algae-based approach to zero-waste digestate management

CSABE | SCGAB Undergraduate Thesis Award, 2025

Anaerobic digestion resolves one waste problem but creates another: digestate, a nutrient-rich residue that requires careful management. Our team designed a system to valorize all of it.

Our client, Biogas-ZE, digests stillage from a Kentucky whiskey distillery and operates under a zero-waste, net-zero emissions mandate. The distillery’s wastewater alone represents roughly 70% of the local municipal plant’s treatment capacity; as such, discharging digestate to the sewer is not a viable option. Over two semesters, our team of four designed the complete treatment train, then built and operated a lab-scale photobioreactor to validate its final, least-proven stage.

Problem
Digestate from a Kentucky distillery’s biogas plant cannot go to the sewer: the distillery’s wastewater alone uses roughly 70% of the municipal plant’s capacity.
My role
Member of a four-person team on the process design. I designed, built and experimentally validated the photobioreactor.
Approach
A four-stage treatment train (centrifuge, vacuum evaporation, ultrafiltration with struvite recovery, and algae polishing), with a six-vessel benchtop reactor to test the least-proven stage.
Result
Orthophosphate below 10 ppm by day 10 in every treatment, 14 mM identified as the stable ammonium loading, and the national CSABE | SCGAB Undergraduate Thesis Award.

System design

A four-stage treatment train

Each stage delivers a cleaner stream to the next while recovering a product that can be sold or reused.

  1. Primary separation

    A decanter centrifuge splits digestate into solids and liquid at 85 to 92% efficiency, with no chemical flocculants and so no extra waste stream.

    Out: wet solids, low-turbidity liquid

  2. Solids processing

    Vacuum evaporation dries the solids at lower temperatures than conventional thermal drying.

    Out: pellets for solid fuel or soil amendment

  3. Liquid processing

    Ceramic ultrafiltration removes particulates, then a fluidized-bed reactor doses magnesium to precipitate struvite.

    Out: struvite, a slow-release fertilizer

  4. Algae polishing

    Microalgae remove the remaining nitrogen and phosphorus, absorb CO₂ from the biogas, and produce biomass that is returned to the digester.

    Out: cleaner water, more biogas

Prototype

Photobioreactor design and construction

Algae polishing was the stage with the least supporting data, so I designed and built a benchtop reactor to cultivate algae in synthetic digestate under controlled conditions. The design progressed through three iterations: narrow-band red-blue LED strips, a rigid broad-spectrum lighting platform, and the final frame shown below.

Vessels
Six 500 mL culture jars running in parallel, enough for two simultaneous trials
Light
Full-spectrum T5 grow lights calibrated to 100 to 105 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at each jar
Carbon
Automated CO₂ delivery, timed to the light cycle, held in the range that boosts growth without inhibiting it
Frame
Aluminum rails and 3D-printed mounts with heat-set threaded inserts, modelled in Fusion 360

Results

Experimental results

Chlamydomonas reinhardtii was cultivated for ten days at three ammonium loadings (7, 14 and 18 mM NH₄Cl), with optical density, pH and orthophosphate measured daily across two trials.

  • 14 mMthe ammonium loading that produced steady, consistent growth
  • 18 mMrapid initial growth, followed by temporary inhibition
  • < 10 ppmorthophosphate by day 10 in all treatments, across both trials

Limitations. The commercial test strips measured un-ionized ammonia; however, at the culture pH nearly all nitrogen was present as ionized ammonium, so the nitrogen readings could not be used. Future trials will require a laboratory method for ammonium to close the nutrient mass balance.
